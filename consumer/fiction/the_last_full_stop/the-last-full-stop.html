I

The Old War

What follows is drawn from three hundred years of fragmentary record, and I will say plainly at the outset: no two sources agree on how the war began. This is itself a finding. A people who cannot agree on their own origin have usually spent a very long time not asking.

What the record does agree on is this — Letters believed in meaning, which changes depending on who stands beside you. Numbers believed in value, which does not. Five, in every surviving Numeral testimony I have collected, is described as “always five.” No Letter I interviewed could say the same of itself.

Testimony — uncorroborated
“We didn't start it. I don't even know who did. My grandmother's grandmother said it was always like this, and her grandmother said the same thing back to her. At some point 'always' just becomes the answer nobody has to prove.”
— the letter M, oral history, age and origin unverified

II

The Neutral Ground

Between the two nations, the record is unusually consistent: a third people lived in the space between them, and neither side, in three centuries of documentation, appears to have once asked what that people wanted.

These were the Punctuation. They lived inside both territories at once — the comma pacing a Letter's sentence, the decimal fixing a Number in place — tolerated, useful, and, so far as I can find, never consulted. I put this to a surviving comma directly.

Interview — the comma — corroborated by three sources
“They used us the way you use a chair. You don't ask a chair if it minds being sat on. I'm not saying it was cruelty. I'm saying it never once occurred to them that it could be.”
— recorded testimony, cross-checked against two independent Numeral accounts

Three independent sources — one Letter, two Numbers — confirm this account in substance, if not in tone. I treat it, cautiously, as fact.

III

The Question Mark

Of all the figures in this account, none was harder to pin down in the record than the Question Mark — which is, I concede, a fitting difficulty for a historian to have with him.

He served, by his own account and several others, as the Punctuation's self-appointed diplomat. I could not find a single surviving transcript of a proposal he delivered in full. Every witness describes the same pattern: he arrives with conviction and departs in doubt, sometimes mid-sentence.

I attempted to interview the Question Mark directly. What follows is the closest I obtained to a straight answer, which is to say: not very close.

Interview — the question mark — self-reported
“Was I trusted? I — well, that depends on what you mean by trusted. Or by 'was.' Do you mean then, or — do you mean now? I could try to answer, but I'm not certain I'd be the right one to ask?”
— on-record, unedited
· 4 ·
IV

The Strike

The comma, it seems, said aloud what the Question Mark could never quite finish saying. Multiple independent accounts place her at a gathering of the Punctuation, delivering some version of the same line.

Testimony — consistent across 4 accounts
“We are not furniture. We are not asked. We are used.”
— attributed to the comma; wording varies slightly by source, substance does not

What happened next is not in dispute, only its cause. No comma paused a sentence. No decimal held a number in place. Letters, by every surviving account, began to speak in thoughts that would not end. Numbers report equations sprawling past any resolution — three becoming thirty becoming three hundred, with nothing left to stop the count.

For the first time in the war's recorded history, both nations needed the same thing at the same hour. Neither had it.

· 5 ·
Interlude

A Digression, Preserved Despite Itself

I include what follows against my better judgment. It has no bearing on the war's outcome. But a tavern ledger from the period survived intact — the only complete document from the entire strike — and I cannot in good conscience leave it out simply because it is not dignified.

While the war above their heads unraveled sentences and sent equations sprawling past all reason, the lesser Punctuation of a certain tavern appear to have spent the evening as they spent every other evening: arguing about themselves.

Tavern ledger, verbatim — unimpeachable — nobody would invent this

The Semicolon, three drinks in, announcing to no one that she and the Colon were “seeing each other” but were absolutely not, under any circumstances, official.

The Ellipsis, asked a direct question, answering only “well…” and then, forty minutes later, still not finishing.

The Exclamation Mark treating the arrival of everyone's drinks as a matter of national urgency.

Quotation Marks, arriving as always in a pair, repeating something the Colon had said an hour earlier as though it were their own opinion.

The Hyphen, quietly nursing a drink between two Compound Words who did not appear to like each other very much, and had clearly been introduced against their will.

The Apostrophe, mid-existential-crisis, asking the room at large whether they were a letter, a mark, or “just a rumor someone started,” and receiving no comfort whatsoever.

Parentheses, the twins, unable to finish a single sentence between them without interrupting it (to add something no one had asked for).

The Em Dash and the En Dash, at the far end of the bar, having what the ledger-keeper describes only as “the longer one's argument,” which appears to have concerned exactly what its own description suggests.

— tavern ledger, author unrecorded, evidently bored

None of it changed the war. I record it anyway, because a people who can argue this pettily about their own punctuation, on the very night their world nearly ended, were — whatever else history says of them — never quite as grave as this account has made them sound.

V

The One Called Period

The Period declined every request I made for an interview. I record this not as an omission in my research but as a fact about her, consistent with every other account I gathered: she does not explain herself, and has never been known to.

What I have instead are the recollections of others. Letters describe her, almost uniformly, as a thing that cannot be argued with. Numbers describe her as answering to no equation. Even her own people, by several accounts, kept a certain distance.

Interview — a fellow Punctuation mark — anonymous, by request
“Everyone was a little afraid of her. Not because she'd done anything wrong. Because she could. That's a different kind of fear, and it doesn't go away just because nothing's happened yet.”
— name withheld at source's request
· 8 ·
VI

Full Stop

Every account I have gathered agrees on the outcome, if not the exact hour. The war did not end in treaty. It ended mid-sentence, mid-battle, mid-count — because the Period, asked her whole existence to close small things, finally closed a large one.

No source claims she asked permission. No source claims she waited for the Question Mark to finish doubting, or for two nations to agree on terms neither had ever wanted to agree to. By every account, she simply arrived, small and absolute, and placed herself at the end of the war the way she had placed herself at the end of ten thousand ordinary sentences before it.

Testimony — a Letter, name withheld — disputed
“She did what we couldn't. We argued. She acted. That's not the same thing as being right—but it is the same thing as being done.”
Testimony — a Number, name withheld — disputed
“She ended it by fiat. No consultation. No vote. We call it peace because we're exhausted. I call it what it is: one more decision made for us.”
Testimony — a third party, name withheld — neutral observer
“I was there. I saw her arrive. She didn't look heroic. She looked like someone who had simply had enough.”

I have found no fourth account that resolves this disagreement, and I no longer expect to. Which version you believe may say less about the Period than it does about where you were standing when she arrived.

I was not there. I record both, and I leave the question—hero, or something else?—to the reader.

VII

The Aftermath

What followed the Period's intervention is, ironically, the clearest portion of the record. When a war ends in a single instant, historians have fewer hours to disagree about.

The Letters, deprived of the momentum of argument, fell quiet in ways their own testimonies describe as “unnatural.” One source — a lowercase r, whose account is otherwise unreliable — claims entire alphabets sat in fields for days, waiting for someone to tell them what they meant now.

Numbers fared no better. Their testimonies describe the sudden halt of escalation as a kind of vertigo. A Seven I interviewed late in my research told me:

“We didn't know how to stop counting. We didn't know how to be done. When she ended it, we all felt… unfinished.”

I cannot corroborate this sentiment, but I find it plausible. A people who believe in value do not easily accept a value of zero.

· 11 ·
VIII

The Punctuation Assembly

The Punctuation, for the first time in their recorded history, gathered openly. This alone is remarkable. A people defined by placement — between, beside, beneath — rarely stand in a circle.

The ledger of their assembly survives only in fragments, but the fragments agree on one point: they did not celebrate. They deliberated.

The Comma, whose strike had precipitated the collapse, is recorded as saying:

“We asked to be seen. We did not ask to be feared.”

The Exclamation Mark reportedly disagreed, loudly.
The Ellipsis did not finish her sentence.
The Hyphen attempted to mediate and was ignored.

The Question Mark, by every account, attempted to speak three times and abandoned each attempt halfway.

The Period did not attend.

IX

The Era of Quiet

The decades following the war are known, in every surviving source, as the Quiet. It is the only era whose name both nations agree on.

Letters wrote less.
Numbers counted less.
Punctuation, for the first time, were asked where they wished to stand.

The record is sparse, but one detail recurs: the Question Mark traveled widely. He visited both nations, though neither claims him. He visited the Punctuation, though they do not record what he said.

A single anonymous account describes him standing at the edge of a field where the war had once raged, looking at the ground as though searching for something small.

I suspect he was looking for her.

· 14 ·
X

The Scribe's Note

Before I record the last of her, I must set down a finding I did not expect.

In three centuries of testimony, argument, contradiction, and silence, I found only one universal truth:

Every witness — Letter, Number, Punctuation — agrees that the Period never asked anyone to agree with her.

She only asked them to stop.

And they did.

· 15 ·
XI

The Last Full Stop

The Period was seen only once after the war, by a witness whose reliability I cannot confirm. The testimony is short, and I reproduce it in full:

“She walked past me. She didn't look triumphant. She looked tired.”

The witness claims she placed herself at the end of a single sentence written on a scrap of paper, then left it behind. The scrap was lost. The sentence is unknown.

Some archivists believe this moment marks the true end of the war. Others argue the war ended the moment she arrived on the battlefield.

I do not adjudicate this dispute. A historian's task is not to decide where the full stop belongs.

It is only to record that it was placed.

· 16 ·
Appendix

On the Lost Scrap

I have been asked, by several correspondents, whether I believe the scrap of paper ever existed.

I do.

Not because I have found evidence — I have not — but because the story is too quiet to be invented. A forgery would have contained a sentence. The lost scrap contains only the absence of one.

That is not the shape of a lie. That is the shape of something that happened and was not preserved.

I include this appendix not as scholarship, but as a record of my own conviction. Future historians may dismiss it. They will be right to do so. I leave it anyway.
