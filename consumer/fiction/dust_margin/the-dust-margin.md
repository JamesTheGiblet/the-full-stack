# The Dust Margin

*A canon-engine-native space western.*

---

## One — The Long Haul

The bay doors groaned open like a man who'd been holding his breath too long.

Jax Marrow walked out onto the docking ring, boot heels clicking against the ribbed metal. The air on Margate Station smelled like recycled oxygen, burnt coffee, and the faint tang of someone else's bad decision. Behind him, The Long Haul sat tethered to the umbilical—a rust-bucket freighter with a water tank the size of a grain silo and an engine that had been held together by prayer for the last six jumps.

The water tank was empty.

Not a drop.

Jax had expected that. He'd flown three systems to deliver a full tank, only to find the buyer had been dead for two months. Nobody told him. Nobody ever told him. So now he was sitting on Margate, a station that had its own water problems, trying to find someone who needed a haul and didn't mind that he was two months late and three systems off course.

The comms crackled in his ear. "Marrow, you're still here?"

"Still here, Alix."

"Did you get paid?"

"Didn't deliver."

"Then why are you still here?"

Jax turned and looked at The Long Haul. The ship's hull was scarred with micrometeorite impacts, the paint worn down to bare metal in streaks. The running lights flickered—port side was losing power again, but he'd left the repair until after the water haul. Funny how that worked.

"I'm thinking," he said.

"Don't hurt yourself."

Alix was the station's dockmaster, a woman whose voice could strip paint. She'd been on Margate for twenty years, long enough to know every ship that docked and every water-hauler who'd ever blown through. She also knew a debt when she saw one.

"You know what happens to water-haulers who don't deliver," she said.

"I know."

"Then why are you thinking? You should be moving."

Jax tapped the comms off and walked back toward the airlock. The ship's ramp lowered with a hydraulic whine that was at least a year past due for maintenance. Inside, the cockpit smelled like oil and something burnt. The nav console was cracked. The water tank's gauge read zero.

He sat down in the pilot's seat and pulled up the local job board.

MARGATE STATION — OPEN HAULS

There was one listing.

"Hauler needed. Cargo: non-perishable. Destination: Ferris Colony. Payment: Three thousand units. Hazard pay included. Contact: Dockmaster Alix."

Three thousand units wasn't water money. But it would get him to Ferris, and Ferris had water. It would also get him out of Margate before the debt caught up with him.

He hit accept.

The comms crackled immediately. "Marrow."

"Yeah."

"That job's got a condition."

"Always does."

"They're not hauling cargo. They want you to take a passenger."

---

## Two — The Passenger

The airlock cycled with a sound like a dying animal.

Jax waited in the cargo bay, arms crossed, watching the inner door. The Long Haul didn't have a passenger cabin. It had a water tank, a cockpit, and a cargo hold that doubled as a sleeping space when he was too tired to make the bunk. Whoever this passenger was, they'd be sleeping on the deck, same as him.

The door opened.

The person who stepped through was small—smaller than he'd expected. Wrapped in a coat that had seen better decades, hood up, face half-hidden. They carried a single bag, worn and patched, and moved like someone who'd spent a long time being careful about where they put their feet.

Jax waited.

The passenger stopped, looked up at him. The hood fell back.

She was young. Not a child—old enough to have a past—but young enough that Jax felt his jaw tighten. She had dark eyes, a bruise on her cheek that was fading to yellow, and the kind of stillness that comes from having learned not to flinch.

"Captain Marrow?" she said.

"That's me."

"I'm—" She stopped. Her hand went to her bag. "I was told you'd just take me. No questions."

"Who told you that?"

"Alix."

Jax closed his eyes for a second. Of course.

"Alix owes me a cut," he said. "She didn't mention you were this young."

"I'm not young," the girl said, with a sudden sharpness. "I'm just not old."

Jax looked at her again. She was dressed clean enough, but the coat didn't fit right. Too big. Someone else's. And the way she held her bag—not like it was valuable, like it was the only thing she had.

"What's your name?" he said.

She hesitated.

"Rena."

It could be true. It could be a lie. Jax had been a pilot long enough to know that either way, it was all he was getting for now.

"Where to, Rena?"

"Ferris Colony," she said. "Like the job says."

"You know what's on Ferris?"

She looked away. "Alix said no questions."

"Alix isn't flying this ship."

The girl said nothing.

Jax sighed. "Alright. No questions. But I need you to understand something." He pointed at the water tank. "That's empty. We're not getting to Ferris on prayers. The job pays three thousand, and that's enough to fill the tank when we get there, but you need to know that if something happens between here and Ferris, there's not a lot I can do about it."

"Something like what?"

"Someone finding you. Someone looking for you."

The girl's face didn't change.

"No one's looking," she said.

That was a lie. Jax knew it. Rena knew he knew it. But she held his gaze anyway, daring him to call it.

He didn't.

"Fine," he said. "Stow your bag. There's a bunk in the back. It's not comfortable, but it's dry."

She nodded and walked past him toward the cockpit. Jax watched her go.

When she was out of earshot, he tapped the comms.

"Alix."

"You got her?"

"I got her."

"Good. Stay off the grid until you hit Ferris."

"Alix. What did you just put on my ship?"

There was a long pause on the other end.

"Something I couldn't keep here," she said. "Now fly, Marrow. Before I change my mind."

The comms clicked off.

Jax looked at the empty water tank. The flickering running lights. The cracked nav console.

And the girl who was already walking into his cockpit like she owned it.

He started the engines.

---

## Three — Trouble in Transit

Three days out from Margate, the water tank was still empty, and the nav console had started making a noise.

Jax knew the noise. It was the same noise his first ship had made before the nav system died mid-jump, leaving him stranded in a dead system for six weeks. He'd been younger then, dumber, and had believed in luck. Now he believed in maintenance schedules and the quiet certainty that everything breaks eventually.

"Captain."

Rena's voice from the cockpit door. She'd been quiet for three days—sat in the corner of the cargo hold, reading a worn datapad, eating rations she'd brought. The bruise on her cheek was fading. She had a new one, smaller, on her wrist.

"Rena."

"Are we on course?"

"Course is fine. Console's not." He tapped the nav display. "It's going to die. Maybe today, maybe tomorrow. We need to make Ferris before it does."

"How far?"

"Day and a half. Two if the drive starts misbehaving."

She came closer, stood beside him. She was small, but she had a way of taking up space that made him feel like he was the one who'd just walked into someone else's cockpit.

"Can you fix it?"

"Not at speed. We'd have to drop out and make repairs. That costs time. Water. And time we don't have if the tank's empty."

She looked at the gauge. Still zero.

"What happens if we don't make Ferris?"

Jax leaned back in the chair. "Then we're drifting. Dead in the water. Someone finds us, maybe. Or we don't get found. The Dust Margin's not a place where people look for you."

She nodded slowly. "You've done this before."

"I've done a lot of things before. Doesn't mean I'm good at them."

The nav console stuttered. The display flickered once, twice, then settled.

"Rena. I need to ask you something."

She tensed.

"Alix said you didn't have questions," she said.

"I'm not Alix. The nav system's going to fail, and if it fails between here and Ferris, we're dead. Not maybe. Dead. So if there's anything you can tell me about why Alix couldn't keep you on Margate, and what's coming behind you, now's the time."

She didn't look away. Her stillness was back. The kind that comes from practice.

"Alix owed someone a favor," she said. "She paid it by putting me on your ship. I don't know who. I don't know why. I just know they wanted me gone."

"Who's 'they'?"

"That's the thing." She turned her wrist, showing the bruise. "I don't know that either. I ran before they could tell me."

Jax looked at the bruise. It wasn't a bruise. It was a mark—three parallel lines, like a claw or a tool. Burned into the skin.

"What is that?"

"I don't know what it is."

"You don't know."

"I ran. The bruise is old. I've had it since I was a kid. I don't know what it is, who made it, or why. I just know it's mine."

The nav console flickered again. The drive hummed lower than it should.

Jax stared at the mark. The three lines. Something about them felt familiar—a feeling, nothing more, one he couldn't place.

"Alright," he said. "I've got two questions left. You can answer them or not. First: is there a chance the nav system holds together to Ferris?"

"Yes."

"Second: is there a chance whatever put that mark on your wrist is what's going to find us before we get there?"

She was quiet for a long time.

"Yes," she said finally. "There's a chance."

Jax turned back to the console.

"Strap in," he said. "We're going faster."

---

## Four — The Nav Fails

The nav console died at 01:47 ship-time.

Jax had been dozing in the pilot's seat, one hand resting on the throttle, when the hum changed. Not the drive—the nav. It went from a steady low whine to a shuddering rattle, and then silence. The display flickered once, twice, and went dark.

He was awake instantly.

"Rena."

She was in the cargo hold, but she heard the change too. Her footsteps came fast.

"What happened?"

"Nav's dead. We're drifting."

He checked the instruments. The drive was still running—thank the stars for small mercies—but without the nav, they were flying blind. No course, no bearing, no way to know if Ferris was ahead of them or behind.

"How long before we can fix it?"

"I can't fix it here. I need parts, and I need a workbench." He looked at the water gauge. Still zero. "We have two options. Option one: we keep the drive running, drift, and hope someone finds us. Option two: we kill the drive, conserve what we have, and pray the nav comes back on its own."

"Which is better?"

"Option one keeps us moving. But we don't know where we're going. Option two keeps us alive longer, but we're dead in the water."

She looked at the blank screen. At the empty gauge. At him.

"What about the mark?"

Jax felt his jaw tighten. "What about it?"

"Whatever put it on my wrist—you said it might find us. What if it already has?"

He didn't answer. Because she was right. The mark had been on her wrist for years, and the nav had died days after she boarded. That wasn't coincidence. The Dust Margin didn't have coincidences. It had debts.

He pulled up the ship's log. The last recorded position was from three hours ago, before the nav died. If he had a general bearing to Ferris, he could keep the drive pointed in the right direction. But without the nav, it was guesswork. And guesswork in the Dust Margin meant death.

"There's someone at Ferris," she said. "Someone I was supposed to meet."

"Who?"

"I don't know. Alix said they'd find me when I arrived."

Jax stared at her. "You're telling me you came all this way—on a ship with no water, a failing nav, and a debt that's following you—to meet a stranger?"

She nodded.

"And you didn't think to mention this before?"

"I didn't think we'd make it."

He wanted to be angry. But she was right. They'd been flying on hope since Margate. Hope was a fuel you rationed, and he'd been burning through it fast.

"Alright," he said. "We keep the drive running. We keep the bearing. And we pray the nav comes back before the tank runs dry."

She nodded again. And then the ship shuddered.

Not the drive. Not the nav. Something else. A low, grinding vibration that came from the hull, like metal scraping against metal.

Jax looked at the instruments. Nothing.

"Rena. Get to the cargo hold."

"What is it?"

"I don't know."

The vibration stopped. And then the comms crackled.

"Captain Marrow."

The voice was calm. Neutral. A woman's voice, measured, like she'd been waiting to speak for a long time.

"You have something on your ship that belongs to me. I'd like it back."

Jax's hand went to the throttle. "Who is this?"

"The owner of the mark."

---

## Five — The Confrontation

The comms crackled again.

"Captain Marrow. I know you can hear me. I'm not going to ask twice."

Jax looked at the instruments. The drive was running. The nav was dead. The water gauge was at zero. And something was out there, attached to his hull, calling him by name.

"How did you know my name?" he said.

"I know everyone who's ever carried water through the Dust Margin. You're not special. You're just the one who took her."

"What do you want?"

"I already told you. I want what's mine."

Jax looked at Rena. She was standing in the cockpit doorway, arms crossed, face pale. She wasn't looking at him. She was looking at the comms panel, like she could see through it to whoever was on the other side.

"Do you know her?" Jax said.

"No."

"She's lying."

"Is she?"

"She's been lying to you since she stepped on your ship. You think she doesn't know the mark? You think she doesn't know who put it there? She knows. She's always known."

Jax turned to Rena. "Is that true?"

She didn't answer.

"Rena."

"I know what it is," she said, quietly. "I don't know who."

"She's lying again."

"Why should I believe you over her?"

There was a pause on the other end. And then the voice said:

"Because you're a water-hauler, Marrow. And she's been running so long she doesn't even remember what she's running from. I'm not chasing her. I'm offering you a choice."

"What choice?"

"Give her to me. I'll give you enough water to get to Ferris. And I'll repair your nav. You walk away clean. She stays with me."

Jax looked at the empty tank.

"You want me to sell her," he said.

"I want you to hand her over. What you call it is your business."

"And if I say no?"

The voice didn't change. It was still calm, measured, like she'd already known the answer.

"Then I take her anyway. And I leave you here. Drifting. Dead. The Dust Margin doesn't forget debts, Marrow. And you just told me which side you're on."

The comms went quiet.

Rena was staring at him.

"Jax."

"Don't."

"Jax, she's telling the truth. I don't know who she is. But she knows what the mark means. That's more than I've ever had."

"Rena."

"If you hand me over, you get to Ferris. You get water. You get to live."

"And you get what?"

She looked away.

"Something I've been running from for a long time."

Jax stood up. Walked to the comms panel. He didn't know who was on the other side. He didn't know if the water was real, or the nav repair, or any of it. But he knew one thing:

"Whoever you are," he said, "I'm not a slaver. And I'm not a debt collector. I'm a water-hauler. That's it. That's all I've ever been."

He killed the comms.

"Rena. Get to the cargo hold. Strap in. We're going to see if the drive can outrun her."

"What about the nav?"

"We don't need a nav to go straight. We just need to go fast."

The drive hummed. Jax pushed the throttle forward. The ship shuddered, groaned, and started to move.

Behind him, something scraped against the hull. And then it was gone.

---

## Six — The Mark

They flew for three hours in silence.

The drive hummed. The nav was dead. The water gauge was at zero. The something that had scraped against the hull hadn't returned, but Jax knew it would. The Dust Margin didn't let go of debts. And Rena was a debt.

He was in the cockpit, staring at the dead nav display, when she came in.

"We can't keep flying like this," she said.

"I know."

"The tank's empty, Jax. We're running on fumes."

"I know."

"You keep saying that."

"Because I know." He turned to look at her. "I know we're dead without water. I know the nav's dead. I know there's something out there that's either going to kill us or take you, and I don't know which is worse. So if you've got something to tell me, Rena, now's the time."

She looked at him for a long moment.

"You want to know what the mark is."

"Yes."

She pulled up her sleeve. The three parallel lines were there, burned into her skin. They looked older now in the dim light. Like they'd been there a long time.

"It's a seal," she said. "A brand. From a place I don't remember. A place I was born."

"Go on."

"I was found. A salvage crew found me in a pod, drifting. I was small. Years old. They thought I was dead. The mark was already on me."

"You don't remember anything about it?"

"No. I remember the pod. I remember the seal. I don't remember the person who put it there."

"Why do you have to run from it?"

"Because I'm not just marked, Jax. I'm a claimant. The seal means I belong to someone. A family. A house. An estate. I don't know which. I don't know who. But someone out there holds the matching seal, and they have a claim on me. That's what the Voice is. They're a collector."

Jax sat back in his chair.

"A collector."

"Yes."

"For who?"

"I don't know. But they're getting closer. They found me on Margate. They'll find me again."

Jax looked at the nav display. Dead. The water gauge. Empty. The drive. Still running. For now.

"How long have you been running?"

"Since the pod. Every time I settle, someone finds me. Every time, I run again. I've been running for twenty years."

He didn't say anything. He just sat there, in the dead cockpit, with a passenger who'd been running since before she could remember.

"Rena."

"Yes."

"The seal. The matching one. If someone holds it, they can claim you. Is that the only way?"

"I don't know."

"Then that's what we're going to find out."

---

## Seven — The Low Point

The drive died at 05:12 ship-time.

Jax had been watching the gauge for the last hour, knowing it was coming. The fuel light had been flickering for days, and the water tank had been empty since Margate. The drive had been running on fumes and hope, and hope was a fuel you rationed.

He was in the cockpit when the hum stopped. The ship went silent. The only sound was the creak of the hull cooling in the void.

"Rena."

She was already at the door.

"I know."

"We're dead in the water."

"How long?"

"Depends on who finds us first. The Voice, or someone who doesn't want to kill us."

She sat down in the co-pilot's seat. Her hands were shaking, but she didn't say anything.

"Rena. The seal. You said someone holds the matching seal."

"Yes."

"Who?"

"I don't know."

"Then how do we find out?"

She was quiet for a long time. And then she said:

"There's a place. A data cache. On Ferris. It was supposed to hold information about the seal. About the family. About who I am."

"Alix didn't tell me that."

"She didn't know. I didn't tell her."

"Why did you tell me?"

She looked at him. Her eyes were dark, and tired, and old in a way that didn't match her face.

"Because we're dead if we don't find it."

Jax leaned back in his chair. The ship was dead. The water was gone. The nav was dead. And the only hope was a data cache on a planet they couldn't reach.

"Alright," he said. "We get to Ferris. We find the cache. We find out who you are."

"Jax. The drive is dead. The nav is dead. The tank is empty."

"I know."

"How are we going to get to Ferris?"

He looked at her. And for the first time since she'd stepped on his ship, he smiled.

"We're going to call for help."

---

## Eight — The Call Answered

The distress signal went out on a broad band, on every channel, no encryption, no hope.

"This is The Long Haul, water freighter, captain Jax Marrow. We are dead in the water. No nav. No drive. No water. Requesting any assistance. Repeat—"

He sent it three times. On the fourth attempt, the comms crackled to life.

"Captain Marrow."

Jax's hand tightened on the comms panel. The voice was calm. Measured. The same voice from before.

"Hello again."

"I heard your call."

"I was hoping someone else would answer."

"Someone else won't. I'm the only one who's been listening for the last hour."

Jax looked at Rena. She was pale, but she was still.

"What do you want?" he said.

"I already told you what I want. I want her. You give her to me, and I give you water. Enough to get to Ferris. Enough to live."

"How do I know you'll keep your word?"

"You don't. But you're dead if you don't."

Jax looked at the empty gauge. The dead nav. The dead drive. The girl who had been running for twenty years.

"Can you give me a minute?" he said.

"You have one."

The comms went quiet.

"Rena."

"I know."

"Whoever they are, whatever they want—if you go with them, I get water. The ship gets water. We both get out."

"Jax."

"I know I'm not a slaver. I know I'm not a debt collector. But I'm a water-hauler, and I have a crew—"

"I'm not your crew."

"You're my passenger. That's closer."

She looked at him. Her eyes were still dark, still tired, but there was something in them that hadn't been there before. Resolve, maybe. Or just the end of running.

"If I go with them," she said. "I can find out who the seal belongs to. I can find out who I am."

"Rena. If you go with them, you might not come back."

"I know."

"Then why are you considering it?"

She was quiet for a moment. And then she said:

"Because I've been running for twenty years. And I'm tired. I want to know whose name is on my arm. Even if it means I don't get to choose what happens next."

Jax didn't say anything. He just looked at her.

And then he keyed the comms.

"She'll go."

---

## Nine — The Exchange

The Voice's ship docked with The Long Haul at 06:33.

Jax stood in the cargo bay, arms crossed, watching the airlock cycle. Rena stood beside him. She was still. Calm. He'd seen that stillness before—it was the same stillness she'd had when she stepped onto the ship, when she'd lied about the mark, when she'd told him she'd been running for twenty years.

It was the stillness of someone who'd already made peace with what came next.

The airlock opened.

The person who stepped through was not what he expected. She was tall, lean, dressed in a dark coat with no insignia. Her face was angular, her hair cropped short, her eyes the color of old metal. She moved like someone who'd spent a long time being careful about where she put her feet.

She looked at Jax. Then at Rena.

"You're taller than I expected," she said.

Rena said nothing.

"I'm Captain Voss. You're the cargo." She turned to Jax. "And you're the water-hauler who doesn't know when to walk away."

"I know," Jax said. "I've been told."

"Good. Then you won't be surprised when I tell you the water's in your tank, and the nav's been patched. Enough to get you to Ferris. No more."

"And Rena?"

"She comes with me."

"Where?"

Voss looked at Rena. "That's not your concern."

"It is if she's my passenger."

"She was your passenger. Now she's my cargo. You made a deal."

Jax looked at Rena. She nodded.

"She comes with me," Voss said again. "And you walk away with your ship. That's the trade."

"I know."

"Then why are you still standing here?"

Jax looked at Rena one last time. She didn't look away. She was still. Calm. Ready.

"Rena."

"Jax."

"If you ever want to come back—"

"I know."

He didn't know if she meant it. He didn't know if she could. He just knew she'd said it.

She walked toward Voss. The airlock closed behind them.

Jax stood in the cargo bay, alone, with a full water tank and a patched nav, and the quiet certainty that he'd just done the right thing.

He didn't feel right.

---

## Ten — The Quiet

Jax stood in the cargo bay for a long time.

The airlock was closed. The tank was full. The nav was patched. The ship was quiet. No humming drive, no creaking hull, no voice from the comms. Just the silence of a ship that had been dead, and was now alive again, and had no cargo.

He walked to the cockpit. Sat down in the pilot's seat. The nav display was lit—green, steady, functional. It showed his position, his bearing, his fuel. Everything he needed.

He looked at the Ferris course.

He could fly it. The nav was patched enough. The water was full enough. He could be there in two days, maybe less. He could find the data cache. He could find out who Rena was, even if she wasn't there to see it.

But that wasn't what he owed.

He had a debt. A real one. The one he'd been running from before Rena ever stepped on his ship. The one Alix knew about, and the Voice had sniffed out, and he'd been avoiding since he first sat in this chair.

You know what happens to water-haulers who don't deliver.

He sat there for a long time.

And then he keyed the comms.

"Ferris Control, this is The Long Haul. Water freighter, inbound. Requesting docking clearance."

The response came after a pause.

"The Long Haul, you're not on the schedule."

"I know. I'm making one."

There was a pause on the other end.

"Why should we let you dock?"

Jax looked at the water gauge. It was full. He had water—enough to make a trade, enough to pay a debt, enough to fill a tank and then some.

"Because I have water," he said. "And I'm willing to sell it cheap."

The pause was longer this time.

"Ferris accepts. Docking bay seven. You have clearance."

Jax cut the comms. He looked at the nav. The course was set. The ship was ready.

He didn't feel ready.

But he'd been flying on hope for so long that he'd forgotten what it felt like to fly on something else.

He pushed the throttle forward.

---

## Eleven — Ferris

Ferris was a dustball with a docking bay.

Jax stepped off The Long Haul onto the docking ring of Ferris Station. The air was thin, recycled, and carried the faint scent of rust and old fuel. The station was small—a single ring, a few bays, a control tower that looked like it had been built out of salvage and hope.

He walked to the market deck.

The market deck was crowded. Haulers, traders, scrappers, and the quiet desperation of people who had come here to buy or sell something they couldn't get anywhere else. Jax walked past stalls of water, food, fuel, salvage. He walked past a woman selling maps of dead systems. He walked past a man selling replacement parts for ships that didn't exist anymore.

He found what he was looking for.

A small stall in the corner, manned by a woman with grey hair and the kind of stillness that came from having seen too much.

"Vera," he said.

She looked up. Her eyes were old, sharp, and they didn't miss anything.

"Jax Marrow. I thought you were dead."

"I'm not."

"No, I can see that." She looked at him. "You owe me water."

"I know."

"Thirty thousand units. You took it from me two years ago. You said you'd pay it back."

"I said I'd pay it back."

"And you didn't."

"I'm paying it back now."

She looked at him for a long moment. And then she said:

"How?"

"I have a full tank. I'll sell it cheap. The money goes to you."

"You're selling water on Ferris?"

"I'm selling water. It's what I do."

She leaned back in her chair. "You're a strange man, Jax Marrow. You run for two years, and then you come back to pay a debt you could have forgotten."

"I don't forget debts."

"I know. That's why I loaned you the water."

Jax nodded. "I'll have the money by tomorrow."

"See that you do."

He turned to leave.

"Jax."

He stopped.

"There's someone looking for you. A woman. Tall, short hair, dark coat. She asked about you yesterday."

Jax felt his chest tighten.

"Did she say why?"

"No. She just asked where you were. And left."

Jax stood there for a moment. Voss had been here. Before he even docked. She'd been looking for him.

"Thank you, Vera."

"Pay the debt, Jax. And maybe next time, don't take so long."

He walked away.

---

## Twelve — The Sale

Jax spent the morning selling water.

He found a buyer quickly—a woman with a cart and a child who needed water more than she needed money. He sold at a loss. Not because he had to, but because he'd been in her place before, and he knew what it felt like to be thirsty.

By noon, he had thirty thousand units.

He walked back to Vera's stall. She was waiting, arms crossed, the same stillness in her eyes.

"Thirty thousand," he said. "All of it."

She looked at the money. Then at him.

"Jax Marrow."

"Vera."

"You didn't have to sell it all."

"I owed it."

## Fifteen — The Decision

"I'm still here."

"Then let me tell you why." Corvus leaned forward. "I brought the girl to Mirage. I transferred her to a ship called The Abeyance. I left that ship here, empty, for you to find. And I sat in this room, waiting for you to walk through that door."

"Why?"

"Because I wanted to meet the water-hauler who refused to sell a passenger."

Jax said nothing.

"I've been watching you, Marrow. From Margate to Ferris to Wayfarer's Reach to here. You've made a lot of choices, and most of them have been the harder ones. I wanted to see if you'd walk into a trap if it meant finding her."

"Are you going to tell me where she is?"

"Yes."

Jax waited.

"But first, I need you to understand something. The girl—Alara—she's not just cargo. She's the heir to a House that has been looking for her for twenty years. They didn't abandon her. They lost her. The pod was supposed to be delivered, and it wasn't. Someone made a choice."

"Kael."

Corvus's eyes flicked toward the door.

"Kael. Yes. He was the marker. He was also the one who left her. He's been running from that choice ever since."

Jax looked at him.

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because I want you to understand what happens next. If you find her, you're not just finding a girl. You're finding a claim. A legal one. One that House Voss has been trying to enforce for two decades."

"And you?"

Corvus leaned back in his chair.

"I'm a broker. I move things. People. Information. I don't care who wins. I care that the transaction happens cleanly. Right now, you're the only one who can deliver her."

"Deliver her to who?"

"To House Voss. Or to herself. That's your choice."

Jax looked at the man behind the desk. The watch that didn't keep time. The ice-colored eyes.

"Why me?"

Corvus smiled.

"Because you're the one who pays his debts."

---

## Thirty — The Choice

Jax stood in the office of The Abeyance, looking at the man who called himself Corvus. The watch that didn't keep time. The ice-colored eyes. The calm assurance of someone who had already calculated every possible outcome.

"You said I have a choice," Jax said.

"I did."

"Deliver her to House Voss, or deliver her to herself."

"Those are the options."

"And you're neutral."

"I'm a broker. I don't care who wins. I care that the transaction is clean."

Jax looked at him for a long moment.

"You've been watching me since Margate. You know what I did. You know what I didn't do."

"I do."

"Then you know I'm not going to deliver her to anyone."

Corvus's expression didn't change.

"I thought you might say that."

"Then why did you offer?"

"Because I wanted to see if you'd take it. And because I wanted to give you the information you need to find her, whether you take the deal or not."

Jax waited.

"She's not on Mirage. She never was. The transfer was a decoy—meant to draw anyone who was following her away from the real destination. The Abeyance was left here to be found. So was the datapad. So was I."

"Where is she?"

Corvus leaned forward.

"She's on a ship called The Last Light. It's a research vessel, old, decommissioned. It's been drifting in the dead zone between systems for months. No one knows it's there. No one's looking for it."

"Why?"

"Because that's where the claim is being settled. House Voss has a representative there. They've been waiting for the girl to be delivered. They don't know she's not coming."

Jax felt the weight of the information.

"You're telling me where she is."

"I'm telling you where she was. She might still be there. She might have been moved. That's the risk you take."

"And House Voss?"

Corvus smiled.

"They'll find out eventually. But by the time they do, you'll have already made your choice."

Jax looked at him.

"Why are you helping me?"

"I'm not helping you. I'm making sure the transaction happens. If you find her first, you'll bring her to the claim. If House Voss finds her first, they'll bring her to the claim. Either way, the claim gets settled. That's all I care about."

Jax turned to leave.

"Jax."

He stopped.

"The woman. The one who gave you the filter. She's not what you think."

Jax looked back.

"Voss?"

"She's a salvager. She's also a liar. She told you she found the pod. She didn't. She was the one who lost it."

Jax felt his chest tighten.

"She was the one who lost it?"

"She was supposed to deliver it. She left it. Same as Kael. She's been carrying that guilt for twenty years, and she's been trying to make it right by finding the girl. But she's never told you the full truth."

Jax said nothing.

"Now you know," Corvus said. "Now you have a choice."

---

## Thirty-One — The Course

Jax walked off The Abeyance in silence.

Kael followed him through the dark corridors of Mirage, past the flickering lights and the empty docking bays, until they reached the ramp of The Last Word. The air was cold, thin, and smelled of ozone. The station hummed its low, uneasy hum.

Jax didn't speak until they were inside the cockpit.

"Set a course for The Last Light."

Kael paused mid-reach for the controls. "You believe him?"

"I believe he wants something. I don't know what it is yet."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I've got." Jax sat in the co-pilot's seat. "He waited for me on an empty ship. He told me where she is. He told me Voss lied. He did all of that after I refused his deal. That's not neutrality. That's a move."

"What kind of move?"

"I don't know yet. But I know he didn't do it to help me."

Kael was quiet for a moment. Then he keyed the nav console.

"The Last Light. Dead zone between systems. It's a long shot."

"It's the only shot."

Kael set the course. The drive hummed to life, and The Last Word lifted off from Mirage, leaving the scarred station behind.

Jax watched the lights recede through the viewport.

"Kael."

"Yeah."

"The Voss thing. What he said about her losing the pod. Do you know anything about that?"

Kael was quiet for a long moment.

"I know she was supposed to deliver it. I know she didn't. I don't know if she lost it or left it. That part was never clear."

"You didn't ask?"

"I didn't want to know."

Jax leaned back in his seat.

"Funny," he said. "Neither did I."

---

## Thirty-Two — The Dead Zone

The dead zone was silent.

No stars. No stations. No light. Just the black of space, unbroken, pressing against the viewport like a held breath. The drive hummed its uneven hum, and the nav display flickered, and Jax sat in the co-pilot's seat watching nothing.

Kael was at the controls, quiet, focused.

"How long?" Jax said.

"Another day. Maybe less if the drive holds."

"And if it doesn't?"

Kael didn't look up. "Then we drift. Dead zone means no help. No signals. No one comes here."

Jax leaned back in his seat.

"You've been here before."

"Once. A job. I didn't stay long."

"Why not?"

"Because the dead zone is where people go to disappear. And I didn't want to disappear."

Jax looked at him.

"Did you ever think about it? Disappearing?"

Kael was quiet for a long moment.

"Every day. It's easier than facing what you left behind."

Jax didn't say anything.

"You know," Kael said, "you could have walked away. After I told you what I did. After Corvus told you about Voss. You could have left Mirage, gone back to Ferris, and forgotten this ever happened."

"I know."

"But you didn't."

"No."

"Why?"

Jax looked at the viewport. The dead black.

"Because she deserves to know who she is. Even if I don't."

Kael nodded slowly.

"I get it. I think."

"Do you?"

Kael met his eyes.

"I think I'm starting to."

The drive hummed. The nav display flickered. And they flew on through the dark.

---

## Thirty-Three — The Last Light

The Last Light was a ghost.

It hung in the dead zone like a forgotten thought—dark, still, its hull scarred by years of drifting. No running lights. No comms. No sign of life. Just a research vessel that had been decommissioned and left to rot in the space between systems.

Jax stood at the viewport of The Last Word, watching it grow larger.

"Looks abandoned," Kael said.

"It is."

"Then why are we here?"

"Because someone told us she's on board."

"And you believe him?"

Jax didn't answer. He just looked at the ship.

"Bring us in. Dock at the main bay."

Kael nodded and guided the ship toward the dark hull. The docking clamps groaned, the airlock cycled, and the two of them stepped onto the cold metal of The Last Light.

The ship was silent.

No hum of engines. No flicker of lights. Just the echo of their footsteps on the metal floor.

Jax walked through the corridors. The research labs were empty. The quarters were empty. The cargo hold was empty.

And then he found her.

She was sitting in a small room at the end of the main corridor, her back against the wall, her knees drawn up to her chest. She was thin, pale, her hair tangled, her clothes worn. But her eyes were the same—dark, still, the kind of stillness that came from having learned not to flinch.

She looked up when he entered.

"Jax."

"Rena."

"You found me."

"I did."

She didn't move. She just sat there, looking at him.

"Are you here to take me back?" she said.

"No."

"Then why are you here?"

Jax knelt down in front of her.

"Because I told you I'd find out who you are. And I did."

He handed her the datapad with the file from the cache.

She looked at it. Then at him.

"Your name is Alara Voss," he said. "You're the heir to House Voss. They didn't abandon you. They lost you. And they've been looking for you ever since."

She stared at the datapad.

"That's not true," she said. "They left me."

"They didn't. Someone made a choice. And that choice was wrong."

She looked up at him.

"Who?"

Jax looked at the doorway.

Kael was standing there. His face was pale, his hands shaking, his eyes fixed on her.

"Someone who's been running from it ever since," Jax said.

Kael stepped forward.

"It was me," he said. "I was the marker. I was supposed to deliver you to House Voss. I didn't. I left you."

Rena looked at him.

"Why?"

Kael's voice cracked.

"Because I was young. Because I was scared. Because I didn't know what they would do to you. I thought someone else would find you. I thought—"

He stopped.

"I've been running from that choice ever since."

Rena looked at him for a long moment.

"You left me to die."

"I didn't—"

"You left me in a pod. In the dark. Alone."

Kael said nothing.

Rena stood up. She was smaller than Jax remembered, thinner, but her stillness was the same.

"I've been running from that moment my whole life," she said. "And you've been running from it too."

She walked toward the door.

"I'm not running anymore."

---

## Thirty-Four — The Representative

Rena stood in the doorway of the small room, her hands at her sides, her eyes fixed on Kael. The silence stretched between them, heavy and unbroken.

Jax watched them both.

"You said House Voss has a representative here," he said.

Kael nodded. "That's what Corvus said."

"Where are they?"

"I don't know. I've been on this ship for a day. I haven't seen anyone."

Jax looked at Rena.

"Have you?"

She shook her head.

"No one. Just a voice on the comms, telling me to stay in this room. They said someone would come for me."

"When?"

"I don't know. They said I'd know when they arrived."

Jax looked at the corridor. Empty. Silent.

"They're here," he said. "They're watching."

Kael tensed.

"How do you know?"

"Because Corvus knew where she was. He knew this ship. And he knew I'd find her. That means someone on this ship is waiting for me to make a choice."

Rena looked at him.

"What choice?"

Jax turned to face her.

"They want you to come back with me. To House Voss. To settle the claim."

"And if I don't?"

"Then we leave. Right now. No claim. No House Voss. No running."

She looked at him.

"That's not a choice," she said.

"It's the only one that matters."

Rena was quiet for a long moment. And then she said:

"I've been running my whole life. I don't want to run anymore. But I don't want to be claimed either."

"Then what do you want?"

She looked at him, and her eyes were still dark, still tired, still old in a way that didn't match her face.

"I want to know who I am. Not whose name is on my arm. Who I am."

Jax looked at her.

"Then come with me. We'll find out together."

She nodded slowly.

"Alright."

---

## Thirty-Five — The Departure

The four of them—Jax, Rena, Kael, and the weight of everything unsaid—walked through the dark corridors of The Last Light toward the docking bay.

Rena moved slowly. Not with fear, but with the careful stillness of someone who had been in one place for too long. She looked at the walls, the floor, the ceiling, like she was memorizing them.

"You don't have to look back," Jax said.

"I'm not looking back. I'm making sure I don't forget."

They reached the airlock. The Last Word was waiting, its hull dark, its ramp lowered.

Jax stopped at the threshold.

"Once we leave, there's no coming back. Not to this ship. Not to this place."

Rena nodded.

"I know."

"And you're sure you want to come with me?"

She looked at him.

"I'm sure."

Jax turned to Kael.

"You stay with the ship. Keep it ready."

Kael nodded and disappeared inside The Last Word.

Jax and Rena stood on the ramp, the dark of the dead zone pressing in around them.

"Where do we go?" she said.

"Anywhere you want. No claim. No House Voss. No running."

She was quiet for a moment.

"I don't know where that is."

"Neither do I. That's the point."

They boarded The Last Word. The ramp closed behind them. The ship lifted off from The Last Light, leaving the dead zone behind.

Jax sat in the pilot's seat, Rena in the co-pilot's seat beside him.

"Where to?" Kael said from the console.

Jax looked at Rena.

"Where do you want to go?"

She looked at the viewport. At the dead zone receding into the distance.

"Somewhere I've never been."

Jax set a course.

---

## Thirty-Six — The Course

The dead zone was behind them.

Jax sat in the pilot's seat of The Last Word, the nav display flickering its uneven flicker, the drive humming its uneven hum. Rena sat beside him, quiet, watching the stars appear as they left the dark behind.

Kael was in the back, checking the ship's systems.

"Where are we going?" Rena said.

"Somewhere safe."

"Is there such a place?"

Jax was quiet for a moment.

"I don't know. But we're going to find out."

She looked at him.

"You came for me. You found me. You could have left me there."

"I could have."

"Why didn't you?"

Jax looked at the nav display. The course was set, but the destination was still forming in his mind.

"Because you deserve to know who you are. And I'm the one who promised to help you find out."

"I told you," she said. "I don't want to know whose name is on my arm."

"Then we'll find out something else."

She was quiet for a moment.

"What?"

"Anything. Everything. What you like. What you don't. Where you want to go. Who you want to be. That's what I can give you. Not a name. A place to start."

She looked at him.

"That's more than anyone's ever offered."

Jax nodded.

"I know."

He set the course for Ferris. Not because it was safe, but because it was the only place he'd ever come close to calling home.

Rena watched the stars pass.

"Jax."

"Yeah."

"What happens when House Voss finds us?"

He looked at her.

"Then we make another choice. Same as always."

---

## Thirty-Seven — Ferris

Ferris Station was the same as he remembered it.

The same smell of recycled air, the same hum of desperate commerce, the same quiet promise of a universe that didn't care if you lived or died. Jax walked through the market deck, Rena beside him, Kael following at a distance.

Vera's stall was in the same corner. She was there, unpacking a crate of water filters, arms covered in dust. She looked up when she saw them.

"Jax Marrow. I told you not to come back."

"I know."

"And you brought company."

"This is Rena. She's the passenger I was looking for."

Vera looked at Rena. Then at Jax.

"You found her."

"I found her."

"And the man who marked her?"

Jax didn't answer.

Vera nodded slowly.

"Alright. I'll ask one question. Do you have somewhere to go?"

"No."

"Then you're staying with me."

Jax looked at her.

"Vera—"

"I didn't say you could stay for free. I'm giving you a place to hide. In exchange, you tell me what happened. The whole story. From Margate to here."

Jax nodded.

"Deal."

---

## Thirty-Eight — The Story

Vera's quarters were small, cluttered, and warm. A single lamp cast a circle of light over the table where Jax sat, Rena beside him, Kael standing by the door.

Vera sat across from him, her arms crossed, her eyes sharp.

"Start at the beginning," she said.

Jax did.

He told her about Margate, about Alix, about the job that wasn't a job. He told her about Rena stepping onto his ship, about the dying nav, about the Voice and the deal and the empty tank. He told her about Ferris the first time, about paying his debt, about Voss finding him and the data cache and the name.

He told her about Mirage. About Kael. About Corvus. About The Last Light.

He left out Kael's confession.

Vera listened without interrupting. When he finished, she was quiet for a long moment.

"You left something out," she said.

Jax didn't answer.

"The man who marked her. You didn't tell me who he was."

Jax looked at Kael. Kael met his eyes and gave a small, almost imperceptible nod.

"It was him," Jax said. "Kael. He was the marker. He left her."

Vera looked at Kael.

"You marked a child."

"I did."

"And you left her."

"I did."

Vera leaned back in her chair.

"Jax Marrow," she said, "you have a talent for finding people who've made terrible choices."

Jax said nothing.

"What do you want from me?" Vera asked.

"I need a place to stay. A place where she can figure out who she is without running."

Vera looked at Rena.

"And what do you want, girl?"

Rena met her eyes.

"I want to stop running."

Vera nodded slowly.

"Alright. You can stay. But I need you to understand something. Ferris isn't safe. It's just where I am. If House Voss finds you here, I can't protect you."

Jax nodded.

"I know."

"Then we have a deal."

---

## Thirty-Nine — The Waiting

Ferris Station was quieter at night.

The market deck was empty, the stalls shuttered, the hum of commerce replaced by the low thrum of the station's aging systems. Jax sat on a crate near Vera's stall, looking out at the dark corridor, Rena beside him.

They'd been there for three days.

Three days of waiting. Three days of watching. Three days of nothing.

"You think they're coming?" Rena said.

"Yes."

"How do you know?"

"Because they let us leave. They're choosing the moment."

She was quiet for a moment.

"What do you think they want?"

"The claim. You. The legal tie that binds you to House Voss."

"And after that?"

Jax looked at her.

"I don't know. That's up to you."

She looked down at her wrist. The mark was still there, three parallel lines, burned into her skin.

"I've been carrying this my whole life," she said. "I don't even know what it means."

"Then we'll find out together."

She looked up at him.

"Jax."

"Yeah."

"Thank you. For coming for me."

He didn't answer. He just sat there, looking out at the dark corridor, waiting.

---

## Forty — The Representative

They came at dawn.

Jax was awake, sitting in the same spot he'd been in for three days, watching the corridor. Rena was asleep in Vera's quarters. Kael was on the ship, waiting.

The footsteps echoed down the empty market deck.

Jax stood.

The person who stepped into the light was tall, lean, dressed in a coat that had no insignia. Their face was angular, their hair cropped short, their eyes the color of stone. They moved like someone who had been waiting a long time and had no intention of rushing now.

"You're Jax Marrow," they said.

"I am."

"I'm the representative of House Voss. I've come to claim the heir."

Jax didn't step closer.

"She's not a cargo."

"No. She's a legal obligation. The seal is valid. The claim is enforceable. House Voss has been waiting for twenty years."

"And you let us leave."

"I let you leave because I wanted to see what you would do."

"And what did you see?"

The representative looked at him.

"I saw a man who would rather protect a stranger than deliver her to a family that's been looking for her. That's rare."

Jax said nothing.

"Where is she?"

"Sleeping."

"Wake her."

"No."

The representative's eyes didn't change.

"You're making a mistake."

"I've made a lot of mistakes. This isn't one of them."

They were quiet for a long moment.

"Fine. I'll wait."

Jax stood in the corridor, facing the representative of House Voss, the claim hanging between them like a held breath.

---

## Forty-One — The Choice

Rena found them in the corridor.

She was still half-asleep, her hair tangled, her eyes still dark and tired. She looked at Jax. Then at the representative. Then back at Jax.

"Who is this?"

"The representative of House Voss."

She didn't flinch.

"You came for me."

"I came to offer you a choice."

Rena looked at Jax.

"You said you wouldn't wake me."

"He didn't. I waited."

Rena turned to face the representative.

"What choice?"

"Come with me. Return to House Voss. Settle the claim. You'll have a name, a place, a future. Or stay here, with him, and remain a claimant forever—never free, never whole, always running."

Rena was quiet for a long moment.

And then she said:

"I've already made my choice."

The representative waited.

"I'm not coming with you. I'm not going to House Voss. I'm staying here, with him, until I figure out who I am. And when I do, if I want to find you, I will. But it will be my choice. Not yours. Not his. Mine."

The representative looked at her.

"You understand what you're giving up."

"I understand what I'm gaining."

They were quiet for a moment.

"Then I'll leave you to it."

They turned and walked away.

Jax stood in the corridor, watching them go. Rena stood beside him.

"You didn't have to do that," he said.

"Yes, I did."

"Why?"

"Because I've been running my whole life. And I'm done."

She looked at him.

"Where do we go now?"

Jax looked at the corridor. At the empty market deck. At the station that had become a hiding place.

"Anywhere you want."

"Anywhere?"

"Anywhere."

She smiled.

"Then let's go."
